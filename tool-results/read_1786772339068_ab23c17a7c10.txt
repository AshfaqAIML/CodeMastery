   474→        <h2>1.1 What Is Computer Science?</h2>
   475→
   476→        <p>
   477→            The name is slightly misleading. Computer Science is not
   478→            primarily the study of computers, just as astronomy is not the
   479→            study of telescopes. Computers are the <em>tool</em>. The subject
   480→            itself is about something deeper:
   481→        </p>
   482→
   483→        <div class="definition">
   484→            <strong>Computer Science:</strong>
   485→            <p>
   486→                The study of <strong>computation</strong> — how problems can
   487→                be solved by following precise, step-by-step procedures, and
   488→                how those procedures can be carried out automatically by
   489→                machines.
   490→            </p>
   491→        </div>
   492→
   493→        <p>
   494→            That definition contains two words we must explain immediately:
   495→            <strong>computation</strong> and <strong>step-by-step
   496→            procedures</strong>.
   497→        </p>
   498→
   499→        <p>
   500→            <strong>Computation</strong> simply means: taking some input,
   501→            following definite rules, and producing an output. When you add
   502→            7 + 5 and get 12, you performed a computation. The input was
   503→            "7 + 5", the rule was addition, and the output was 12.
   504→        </p>
   505→
   506→        <p>
   507→            A <strong>step-by-step procedure</strong> for solving a problem
   508→            has a technical name: an <strong>algorithm</strong>.
   509→        </p>
   510→
   511→        <div class="definition">
   512→            <strong>Algorithm:</strong>
   513→            <p>
   514→                A precise, finite sequence of steps that solves a problem.
   515→                Each step must be clear, and the procedure must eventually
   516→                finish.
   517→            </p>
   518→        </div>
   519→
   520→        <aside class="callout callout-analogy">
   521→            <strong>Real-World Analogy</strong>
   522→            <p>
   523→                An algorithm is like a recipe. A recipe lists exact steps in
   524→                an exact order: crack the eggs, whisk them, heat the pan,
   525→                pour, cook. If someone follows the recipe precisely, they
   526→                get the same result every time. An algorithm is a "recipe"
   527→                for solving a problem — and unlike a human cook, a computer
   528→                follows the recipe <em>exactly</em>, with no improvisation.
   529→            </p>
   530→        </aside>
   531→
   532→        <p>
   533→            So a more complete picture of Computer Science is:
   534→        </p>
   535→
   536→        <ul>
   537→            <li><strong>Designing algorithms</strong> — finding good step-by-step solutions to problems.</li>
   538→            <li><strong>Representing information</strong> — figuring out how numbers, text, images, and sound can be stored and processed.</li>
   539→            <li><strong>Building systems</strong> — organizing hardware and software so computations happen reliably and efficiently.</li>
   540→            <li><strong>Understanding limits</strong> — knowing what can be computed, what cannot, and how fast.</li>
   541→        </ul>
   542→
   543→        <aside class="callout callout-important">
   544→            <strong>Key idea:</strong>
   545→            Computer Science is fundamentally about
   546→            <strong>problem solving</strong>. Programming (writing code) is
   547→            one of its most important tools, but the thinking comes first.
   548→            A person who can code but cannot design solutions is a tool
   549→            operator; a computer scientist designs the solution and then
   550→            uses code to express it.
   551→        </aside>
   552→
   553→        <h2>1.2 What Is Computing?</h2>
   554→
   555→        <p>
   556→            <strong>Computing</strong> is the act of performing computation
   557→            — especially with the help of machines. Humans computed long
   558→            before computers existed: accountants computed taxes, navigators
   559→            computed positions using stars, and teams of people called
   560→            "computers" (yes, that word once described a job title)
   561→            calculated artillery tables by hand.
   562→        </p>
   563→
   564→        <p>
   565→            Modern computing means <strong>automating</strong> that process:
   566→            instead of a person doing each step, we record the steps once
   567→            and let a fast, tireless machine repeat them billions of times
   568→            per second.
   569→        </p>
   570→
   571→        <p>
   572→            This is the core value of computing: once you turn a task into
   573→            an algorithm, a machine can repeat it forever without getting
   574→            tired, bored, or careless — as long as the instructions are
   575→            correct.
   576→        </p>
   577→
   578→        <h2>1.3 What Is a Computer?</h2>
   579→
   580→        <p>
   581→            Now we can define the machine itself precisely.
   582→        </p>
   583→
   584→        <div class="definition">
   585→            <strong>Computer:</strong>
   586→            <p>
   587→                An electronic machine that accepts <strong>data</strong>
   588→                (input), follows stored instructions called a
   589→                <strong>program</strong> to process that data, and produces
   590→                results (output).
   591→            </p>
   592→        </div>
   593→
   594→        <p>Four terms in that definition need immediate explanation:</p>
   595→
   596→        <dl class="definitions">
   597→            <dt>Data</dt>
   598→            <dd>
   599→                Raw facts and values: numbers, text, measurements, clicks,
   600→                temperatures. Data by itself has no meaning yet — it becomes
   601→                meaningful when processed. (We explore this fully in
   602→                Part 4.)
   603→            </dd>
   604→
   605→            <dt>Input</dt>
   606→            <dd>
   607→                Any data entering the computer: a key press, a mouse
   608→                movement, a voice recording, a temperature reading.
   609→            </dd>
   610→
   611→            <dt>Output</dt>
   612→            <dd>
   613→                Any result the computer produces: text on a screen, sound
   614→                from a speaker, a sent message, a printed page.
   615→            </dd>
   616→
   617→            <dt>Program</dt>
   618→            <dd>
   619→                A stored list of instructions telling the computer what to
   620→                do with the input, step by step.
   621→            </dd>
   622→        </dl>
   623→
   624→        <p>Two more terms you will see constantly:</p>
   625→
   626→        <dl class="definitions">
   627→            <dt>Hardware</dt>
   628→            <dd>
   629→                The physical parts of a computer you can touch: chips,
   630→                wires, screens, keyboards.
   631→            </dd>
   632→
   633→            <dt>Software</dt>
   634→            <dd>
   635→                The intangible instructions that run on the hardware:
   636→                operating systems, apps, games. Software is "soft" because
   637→                it can be changed without touching the machine physically.
   638→            </dd>
   639→        </dl>
   640→
   641→        <p>
   642→            Every computer, from the largest server to the smallest smart
   643→            watch, follows the same basic pattern:
   644→        </p>
   645→
   646→        <pre class="ascii-diagram">
   647→Input  →  Processing  →  Output
   648→             ↑
   649→          Storage
   650→        </pre>
   651→
   652→        <p>
   653→            <strong>Storage</strong> is where data and programs are kept,
   654→            either temporarily while working or permanently for later use.
   655→            This simple diagram is the skeleton of every computer system you
   656→            will ever study. Part 3 expands it in detail.
   657→        </p>
   658→
   659→        <aside class="callout callout-tip">
   660→            <strong>Important:</strong>
   661→            A computer is <em>not</em> only a laptop or desktop. The chips
   662→            inside cars, televisions, washing machines, routers, and traffic
   663→            lights are computers too. When a computer is built into another
   664→            device to control it, we call it an
   665→            <strong>embedded computer</strong> — "embedded" meaning built
   666→            into something else.
   667→        </aside>
   668→
   669→        <h2>1.4 What Does a Computer Scientist Do?</h2>
   670→
   671→        <p>
   672→            Computer scientists use the ideas above to solve real problems.
   673→            Their work typically falls into a few categories:
   674→        </p>
   675→
   676→        <ul>
   677→            <li>
   678→                <strong>Designing algorithms and software</strong> — creating
   679→                the step-by-step logic behind search engines, games, banking
   680→                systems, and medical tools.
   681→            </li>
   682→            <li>
   683→                <strong>Building systems</strong> — making many computers and
   684→                programs work together reliably: networks, databases, cloud
   685→                platforms.
   686→            </li>
   687→            <li>
   688→                <strong>Working with data and intelligence</strong> — building
   689→                systems that learn patterns from data (machine learning) and
   690→                systems that understand or generate language and images (AI).
   691→            </li>
   692→            <li>
   693→                <strong>Research</strong> — discovering what is computable,
   694→                how to make computation faster, safer, or smaller.
   695→            </li>
   696→            <li>
   697→                <strong>Applying computing to other fields</strong> — biology
   698→                (analyzing DNA), finance (modeling markets), climate science
   699→                (simulating weather), art (generative tools).
   700→            </li>
   701→        </ul>
   702→
   703→        <p>
   704→            Notice that "typing code" is only a small visible slice of this
   705→            work. Most of the real work happens <em>before</em> code:
   706→            understanding the problem, designing a solution, and choosing
   707→            the right structures and trade-offs.
   708→        </p>
   709→
   710→        <h2>1.5 What Can Computers Do?</h2>
   711→
   712→        <p>Computers are extraordinarily good at a specific set of things:</p>
   713→
   714→        <ul>
   715→            <li>
   716→                <strong>Arithmetic at extreme speed</strong> — billions of
   717→                calculations per second.
   718→            </li>
   719→            <li>
   720→                <strong>Storing and retrieving data</strong> — huge amounts,
   721→                with near-perfect recall.
   722→            </li>
   723→            <li>
   724→                <strong>Repeating tasks without fatigue</strong> — the same
   725→                operation a million times with no loss of accuracy.
   726→            </li>
   727→            <li>
   728→                <strong>Communication</strong> — moving data across the world
   729→                in milliseconds.
   730→            </li>
   731→            <li>
   732→                <strong>Automation</strong> — performing scheduled or
   733→                triggered actions without human involvement.
   734→            </li>
   735→            <li>
   736→                <strong>Simulation</strong> — modeling weather, crashes,
   737→                economies, or neural networks before doing them in reality.
   738→            </li>
   739→            <li>
   740→                <strong>Pattern recognition</strong> — with machine learning,
   741→                finding patterns in data that are difficult to hand-code.
   742→            </li>
   743→        </ul>
   744→
   745→        <h2>1.6 What Can Computers NOT Do?</h2>
   746→
   747→        <p>
   748→            Understanding limitations is just as important as understanding
   749→            capabilities. Five limits matter most for beginners:
   750→        </p>
   751→
   752→        <ol>
   753→            <li>
   754→                <strong>Computers only follow instructions.</strong>
   755→                If the instructions are wrong or ambiguous, the result is
   756→                wrong. Computers do not "guess what you meant." There is a
   757→                famous phrase for this: <em>garbage in, garbage out</em> —
   758→                bad input or bad instructions produce bad results.
   759→            </li>
   760→            <li>
   761→                <strong>Computers depend on the data they are given.</strong>
   762→                A system trained on biased or incomplete data will produce
   763→                biased or incomplete decisions.
   764→            </li>
   765→            <li>
   766→                <strong>Some problems are unsolvable by any computer.</strong>
   767→                At a basic level, certain questions are proven to have no
   768→                possible algorithm that always answers them correctly. You
   769→                will meet this idea if you later study the theory of
   770→                computation. For now, just know: computation has hard
   771→                mathematical limits.
   772→            </li>
   773→            <li>
   774→                <strong>Physical limits exist.</strong>
   775→                Signals cannot travel faster than light, chips produce heat,
   776→                and components can only be made so small. These limits shape
   777→                how fast computers can become.
   778→            </li>
   779→            <li>
   780→                <strong>Computers do not truly understand.</strong>
   781→                Modern AI can generate impressive text and images, but it
   782→                works by recognizing and producing patterns in data. It does
   783→                not have beliefs, intentions, or genuine understanding the
   784→                way a person does. Keeping this distinction in mind will
   785→                help you reason clearly about AI in Parts 33 and 34.
   786→            </li>
   787→        </ol>
   788→
   789→        <h2>1.7 Why Computer Science Matters</h2>
   790→
   791→        <p>
   792→            Computing is now the backbone of nearly every field:
   793→        </p>
   794→
   795→        <ul>
   796→            <li><strong>Medicine</strong> — imaging, diagnostics, drug discovery.</li>
   797→            <li><strong>Finance</strong> — payments, fraud detection, trading systems.</li>
   798→            <li><strong>Communication</strong> — messaging, video calls, social platforms.</li>
   799→            <li><strong>Science</strong> — climate modeling, genomics, space exploration.</li>
   800→            <li><strong>Entertainment</strong> — games, streaming, recommendation systems.</li>
   801→            <li><strong>Transportation</strong> — navigation, traffic control, autonomous vehicles.</li>
   802→        </ul>
   803→
   804→        <p>
   805→            For you personally, this foundation matters because every
   806→            advanced role you might target — backend engineer, data
   807→            scientist, ML engineer, AI agent engineer — is built on these
   808→            concepts. Engineers who understand foundations learn new tools
   809→            faster, debug deeper, and design better systems.
   810→        </p>

   812→        <h2>1.8 Computer Science vs Related Fields</h2>
   813→
   814→        <p>
   815→            Several fields overlap with Computer Science, and beginners often
   816→            mix them up. Let's define each one before comparing them.
   817→        </p>
   818→
   819→        <dl class="definitions">
   820→            <dt>Computer Science (CS)</dt>
   821→            <dd>
   822→                The study of computation itself: algorithms, data, systems,
   823→                and what can be automated. It is the theoretical and
   824→                practical core from which the others grow.
   825→            </dd>
   826→
   827→            <dt>Computer Engineering (CE)</dt>
   828→            <dd>
   829→                The design of physical computer systems: circuits, chips,
   830→                processors, and the boundary where hardware meets software.
   831→                A computer engineer might design the chip inside a phone.
   832→            </dd>
   833→
   834→            <dt>Information Technology (IT)</dt>
   835→            <dd>
   836→                The use and management of computer systems to support
   837→                organizations: setting up networks, maintaining servers,
   838→                supporting users, keeping systems secure and running.
   839→            </dd>
   840→
   841→            <dt>Software Engineering (SE)</dt>
   842→            <dd>
   843→                The disciplined practice of building reliable, maintainable
   844→                software in teams: requirements, design, testing, version
   845→                control, deployment, and maintenance. We cover its
   846→                foundations in Part 26.
   847→            </dd>
   848→
   849→            <dt>Data Science (DS)</dt>
   850→            <dd>
   851→                The practice of extracting knowledge from data, combining
   852→                programming, statistics, and domain expertise — for example,
   853→                analyzing customer behavior to predict sales.
   854→            </dd>
   855→
   856→            <dt>Artificial Intelligence (AI)</dt>
   857→            <dd>
   858→                A branch of Computer Science focused on building systems that
   859→                perform tasks normally requiring human-like intelligence:
   860→                recognizing speech, making decisions, generating text.
   861→                Machine learning is its most important modern approach.
   862→            </dd>
   863→        </dl>

   933→        <h2>1.9 The Major Areas of Computer Science</h2>
   934→
   935→        <p>

   939→
   940→        <ul>

   943→                what can and cannot be computed, and how efficiently.
   944→            </li>
   945→            <li>
   946→                <strong>Algorithms and data structures</strong> — efficient

   948→                (Parts 16–19).
   949→            </li>
   950→            <li>
   951→                <strong>Programming languages</strong> — how humans write
   952→                instructions computers can eventually execute (Parts 14–15).
   953→            </li>
   954→            <li>
   955→                <strong>Computer systems</strong> — hardware architecture,
   956→                operating systems, and networks (Parts 7–12, 20, 22).
   957→            </li>
   958→            <li>
   959→                <strong>Databases</strong> — storing, organizing, and
   960→                querying large amounts of data reliably (Part 24).
   961→            </li>
   962→            <li>
   963→                <strong>Artificial intelligence and machine learning</strong>
   964→                — systems that learn from data (Parts 33–34).
   965→            </li>
   966→            <li>
   967→                <strong>Security</strong> — protecting systems and data from
   968→                attacks and mistakes (Part 25).
   969→            </li>
   970→            <li>
   971→                <strong>Human-computer interaction and graphics</strong> —
   972→                how people experience and visualize computing.
   973→            </li>
   974→            <li>
   975→                <strong>Software engineering</strong> — building software
   976→                systematically in teams (Part 26).
   977→            </li>
   978→        </ul>

   980→        <h2>1.10 How the Areas Connect</h2>
   981→
   982→        <p>
   983→            These areas are not isolated. They stack on each other:
   984→        </p>
   985→
   986→        <pre class="ascii-diagram">
   987→Theory (what is computable?)
   988→   ↓
   989→Algorithms (how do we solve problems?)
   990→   ↓
   991→Programming languages (how do we express solutions?)
   992→   ↓
   993→Systems: OS, networks, databases (where do solutions run?)
   994→   ↓
   995→Applications: AI, security, web, mobile (what do we build?)
   996→        </pre>
   997→
   998→        <p>
   999→            Every application at the top — an AI chatbot, a banking app, a
  1000→            video game — ultimately depends on every layer beneath it. When
  1001→            you understand the layers, complex systems stop being mysterious.
  1002→        </p>

  1168→
  1169→        <h2>Summary</h2>
  1170→
  1171→        <ul>
  1172→            <li>Computer Science is the study of computation and problem solving, not merely the study of computers.</li>
  1173→            <li>An algorithm is a precise, finite sequence of steps that solves a problem.</li>
  1174→            <li>A computer accepts input, follows a stored program, and produces output.</li>
  1175→            <li>Hardware is the physical machine; software is the instructions that run on it.</li>
  1176→            <li>Computers excel at speed, repetition, storage, and communication, but they only follow instructions and have hard limits.</li>
  1177→            <li>CS, Computer Engineering, IT, Software Engineering, Data Science, and AI are related but distinct fields.</li>
  1178→            <li>The areas of CS stack on each other: theory → algorithms → programming → systems → applications.</li>
  1179→        </ul>
