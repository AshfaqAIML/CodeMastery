    56→   474→        <h2>1.1 What Is Computer Science?</h2>
    57→   475→
    58→   476→        <p>
    59→   477→            The name is slightly misleading. Computer Science is not
    60→   478→            primarily the study of computers, just as astronomy is not the
    61→   479→            study of telescopes. Computers are the <em>tool</em>. The subject
    62→   480→            itself is about something deeper:
    63→   481→        </p>
    64→   482→
    65→   483→        <div class="definition">
    66→   484→            <strong>Computer Science:</strong>
    67→   485→            <p>
    68→   486→                The study of <strong>computation</strong> — how problems can
    69→   487→                be solved by following precise, step-by-step procedures, and
    70→   488→                how those procedures can be carried out automatically by
    71→   489→                machines.
    72→   490→            </p>
    73→   491→        </div>
    74→   492→
    75→   493→        <p>
    76→   494→            That definition contains two words we must explain immediately:
    77→   495→            <strong>computation</strong> and <strong>step-by-step
    78→   496→            procedures</strong>.
    79→   497→        </p>
    80→   498→
    81→   499→        <p>
    82→   500→            <strong>Computation</strong> simply means: taking some input,
    83→   501→            following definite rules, and producing an output. When you add
    84→   502→            7 + 5 and get 12, you performed a computation. The input was
    85→   503→            "7 + 5", the rule was addition, and the output was 12.
    86→   504→        </p>
    87→   505→
    88→   506→        <p>
    89→   507→            A <strong>step-by-step procedure</strong> for solving a problem
    90→   508→            has a technical name: an <strong>algorithm</strong>.
    91→   509→        </p>
    92→   510→
    93→   511→        <div class="definition">
    94→   512→            <strong>Algorithm:</strong>
    95→   513→            <p>
    96→   514→                A precise, finite sequence of steps that solves a problem.
    97→   515→                Each step must be clear, and the procedure must eventually
    98→   516→                finish.
    99→   517→            </p>
   100→   518→        </div>
   101→   519→
   102→   520→        <aside class="callout callout-analogy">
   103→   521→            <strong>Real-World Analogy</strong>
   104→   522→            <p>
   105→   523→                An algorithm is like a recipe. A recipe lists exact steps in
   106→   524→                an exact order: crack the eggs, whisk them, heat the pan,
   107→   525→                pour, cook. If someone follows the recipe precisely, they
   108→   526→                get the same result every time. An algorithm is a "recipe"
   109→   527→                for solving a problem — and unlike a human cook, a computer
   110→   528→                follows the recipe <em>exactly</em>, with no improvisation.
   111→   529→            </p>
   112→   530→        </aside>
   113→   531→
   114→   532→        <p>
   115→   533→            So a more complete picture of Computer Science is:
   116→   534→        </p>
   117→   535→
   118→   536→        <ul>
   119→   537→            <li><strong>Designing algorithms</strong> — finding good step-by-step solutions to problems.</li>
   120→   538→            <li><strong>Representing information</strong> — figuring out how numbers, text, images, and sound can be stored and processed.</li>
   121→   539→            <li><strong>Building systems</strong> — organizing hardware and software so computations happen reliably and efficiently.</li>
   122→   540→            <li><strong>Understanding limits</strong> — knowing what can be computed, what cannot, and how fast.</li>
   123→   541→        </ul>
   124→   542→
   125→   543→        <aside class="callout callout-important">
   126→   544→            <strong>Key idea:</strong>
   127→   545→            Computer Science is fundamentally about
   128→   546→            <strong>problem solving</strong>. Programming (writing code) is
   129→   547→            one of its most important tools, but the thinking comes first.
   130→   548→            A person who can code but cannot design solutions is a tool
   131→   549→            operator; a computer scientist designs the solution and then
   132→   550→            uses code to express it.
   133→   551→        </aside>
   134→   552→
   135→   553→        <h2>1.2 What Is Computing?</h2>
   136→   554→
   137→   555→        <p>
   138→   556→            <strong>Computing</strong> is the act of performing computation
   139→   557→            — especially with the help of machines. Humans computed long
   140→   558→            before computers existed: accountants computed taxes, navigators
   141→   559→            computed positions using stars, and teams of people called
   142→   560→            "computers" (yes, that word once described a job title)
   143→   561→            calculated artillery tables by hand.
   144→   562→        </p>
   145→   563→
   146→   564→        <p>
   147→   565→            Modern computing means <strong>automating</strong> that process:
   148→   566→            instead of a person doing each step, we record the steps once
   149→   567→            and let a fast, tireless machine repeat them billions of times
   150→   568→            per second.
   151→   569→        </p>
   152→   570→
   153→   571→        <p>
   154→   572→            This is the core value of computing: once you turn a task into
   155→   573→            an algorithm, a machine can repeat it forever without getting
   156→   574→            tired, bored, or careless — as long as the instructions are
   157→   575→            correct.
   158→   576→        </p>
   159→   577→
   160→   578→        <h2>1.3 What Is a Computer?</h2>
   161→   579→
   162→   580→        <p>
   163→   581→            Now we can define the machine itself precisely.
   164→   582→        </p>
   165→   583→
   166→   584→        <div class="definition">
   167→   585→            <strong>Computer:</strong>
   168→   586→            <p>
   169→   587→                An electronic machine that accepts <strong>data</strong>
   170→   588→                (input), follows stored instructions called a
   171→   589→                <strong>program</strong> to process that data, and produces
   172→   590→                results (output).
   173→   591→            </p>
   174→   592→        </div>
   175→   593→
   176→   594→        <p>Four terms in that definition need immediate explanation:</p>
   177→   595→
   178→   596→        <dl class="definitions">
   179→   597→            <dt>Data</dt>
   180→   598→            <dd>
   181→   599→                Raw facts and values: numbers, text, measurements, clicks,
   182→   600→                temperatures. Data by itself has no meaning yet — it becomes
   183→   601→                meaningful when processed. (We explore this fully in
   184→   602→                Part 4.)
   185→   603→            </dd>
   186→   604→
   187→   605→            <dt>Input</dt>
   188→   606→            <dd>
   189→   607→                Any data entering the computer: a key press, a mouse
   190→   608→                movement, a voice recording, a temperature reading.
   191→   609→            </dd>
   192→   610→
   193→   611→            <dt>Output</dt>
   194→   612→            <dd>
   195→   613→                Any result the computer produces: text on a screen, sound
   196→   614→                from a speaker, a sent message, a printed page.
   197→   615→            </dd>
   198→   616→
   199→   617→            <dt>Program</dt>
   200→   618→            <dd>
   201→   619→                A stored list of instructions telling the computer what to
   202→   620→                do with the input, step by step.
   203→   621→            </dd>
   204→   622→        </dl>
   205→   623→
   206→   624→        <p>Two more terms you will see constantly:</p>
   207→   625→
   208→   626→        <dl class="definitions">
   209→   627→            <dt>Hardware</dt>
   210→   628→            <dd>
   211→   629→                The physical parts of a computer you can touch: chips,
   212→   630→                wires, screens, keyboards.
   213→   631→            </dd>
   214→   632→
   215→   633→            <dt>Software</dt>
   216→   634→            <dd>
   217→   635→                The intangible instructions that run on the hardware:
   218→   636→                operating systems, apps, games. Software is "soft" because
   219→   637→                it can be changed without touching the machine physically.
   220→   638→            </dd>
   221→   639→        </dl>
   222→   640→
   223→   641→        <p>
   224→   642→            Every computer, from the largest server to the smallest smart
   225→   643→            watch, follows the same basic pattern:
   226→   644→        </p>
   227→   645→
   228→   646→        <pre class="ascii-diagram">
   229→   647→Input  →  Processing  →  Output
   230→   648→             ↑
   231→   649→          Storage
   232→   650→        </pre>
   233→   651→
   234→   652→        <p>
   235→   653→            <strong>Storage</strong> is where data and programs are kept,
   236→   654→            either temporarily while working or permanently for later use.
   237→   655→            This simple diagram is the skeleton of every computer system you
   238→   656→            will ever study. Part 3 expands it in detail.
   239→   657→        </p>
   240→   658→
   241→   659→        <aside class="callout callout-tip">
   242→   660→            <strong>Important:</strong>
   243→   661→            A computer is <em>not</em> only a laptop or desktop. The chips
   244→   662→            inside cars, televisions, washing machines, routers, and traffic
   245→   663→            lights are computers too. When a computer is built into another
   246→   664→            device to control it, we call it an
   247→   665→            <strong>embedded computer</strong> — "embedded" meaning built
   248→   666→            into something else.
   249→   667→        </aside>
   250→   668→
   251→   669→        <h2>1.4 What Does a Computer Scientist Do?</h2>
   252→   670→
   253→   671→        <p>
   254→   672→            Computer scientists use the ideas above to solve real problems.
   255→   673→            Their work typically falls into a few categories:
   256→   674→        </p>
   257→   675→
   258→   676→        <ul>
   259→   677→            <li>
   260→   678→                <strong>Designing algorithms and software</strong> — creating
   261→   679→                the step-by-step logic behind search engines, games, banking
   262→   680→                systems, and medical tools.
   263→   681→            </li>
   264→   682→            <li>
   265→   683→                <strong>Building systems</strong> — making many computers and
   266→   684→                programs work together reliably: networks, databases, cloud
   267→   685→                platforms.
   268→   686→            </li>
   269→   687→            <li>
   270→   688→                <strong>Working with data and intelligence</strong> — building
   271→   689→                systems that learn patterns from data (machine learning) and
   272→   690→                systems that understand or generate language and images (AI).
   273→   691→            </li>
   274→   692→            <li>
   275→   693→                <strong>Research</strong> — discovering what is computable,
   276→   694→                how to make computation faster, safer, or smaller.
   277→   695→            </li>
   278→   696→            <li>
   279→   697→                <strong>Applying computing to other fields</strong> — biology
   280→   698→                (analyzing DNA), finance (modeling markets), climate science
   281→   699→                (simulating weather), art (generative tools).
   282→   700→            </li>
   283→   701→        </ul>
   284→   702→
   285→   703→        <p>
   286→   704→            Notice that "typing code" is only a small visible slice of this
   287→   705→            work. Most of the real work happens <em>before</em> code:
   288→   706→            understanding the problem, designing a solution, and choosing
   289→   707→            the right structures and trade-offs.
   290→   708→        </p>
   291→   709→
   292→   710→        <h2>1.5 What Can Computers Do?</h2>
   293→   711→
   294→   712→        <p>Computers are extraordinarily good at a specific set of things:</p>
   295→   713→
   296→   714→        <ul>
   297→   715→            <li>
   298→   716→                <strong>Arithmetic at extreme speed</strong> — billions of
   299→   717→                calculations per second.
   300→   718→            </li>
   301→   719→            <li>
   302→   720→                <strong>Storing and retrieving data</strong> — huge amounts,
   303→   721→                with near-perfect recall.
   304→   722→            </li>
   305→   723→            <li>
   306→   724→                <strong>Repeating tasks without fatigue</strong> — the same
   307→   725→                operation a million times with no loss of accuracy.
   308→   726→            </li>
   309→   727→            <li>
   310→   728→                <strong>Communication</strong> — moving data across the world
   311→   729→                in milliseconds.
   312→   730→            </li>
   313→   731→            <li>
   314→   732→                <strong>Automation</strong> — performing scheduled or
   315→   733→                triggered actions without human involvement.
   316→   734→            </li>
   317→   735→            <li>
   318→   736→                <strong>Simulation</strong> — modeling weather, crashes,
   319→   737→                economies, or neural networks before doing them in reality.
   320→   738→            </li>
   321→   739→            <li>
   322→   740→                <strong>Pattern recognition</strong> — with machine learning,
   323→   741→                finding patterns in data that are difficult to hand-code.
   324→   742→            </li>
   325→   743→        </ul>
   326→   744→
   327→   745→        <h2>1.6 What Can Computers NOT Do?</h2>
   328→   746→
   329→   747→        <p>
   330→   748→            Understanding limitations is just as important as understanding
   331→   749→            capabilities. Five limits matter most for beginners:
   332→   750→        </p>
   333→   751→
   334→   752→        <ol>
   335→   753→            <li>
   336→   754→                <strong>Computers only follow instructions.</strong>
   337→   755→                If the instructions are wrong or ambiguous, the result is
   338→   756→                wrong. Computers do not "guess what you meant." There is a
   339→   757→                famous phrase for this: <em>garbage in, garbage out</em> —
   340→   758→                bad input or bad instructions produce bad results.
   341→   759→            </li>
   342→   760→            <li>
   343→   761→                <strong>Computers depend on the data they are given.</strong>
   344→   762→                A system trained on biased or incomplete data will produce
   345→   763→                biased or incomplete decisions.
   346→   764→            </li>
   347→   765→            <li>
   348→   766→                <strong>Some problems are unsolvable by any computer.</strong>
   349→   767→                At a basic level, certain questions are proven to have no
   350→   768→                possible algorithm that always answers them correctly. You
   351→   769→                will meet this idea if you later study the theory of
   352→   770→                computation. For now, just know: computation has hard
   353→   771→                mathematical limits.
   354→   772→            </li>
   355→   773→            <li>
   356→   774→                <strong>Physical limits exist.</strong>
   357→   775→                Signals cannot travel faster than light, chips produce heat,
   358→   776→                and components can only be made so small. These limits shape
   359→   777→                how fast computers can become.
   360→   778→            </li>
   361→   779→            <li>
   362→   780→                <strong>Computers do not truly understand.</strong>
   363→   781→                Modern AI can generate impressive text and images, but it
   364→   782→                works by recognizing and producing patterns in data. It does
   365→   783→                not have beliefs, intentions, or genuine understanding the
   366→   784→                way a person does. Keeping this distinction in mind will
   367→   785→                help you reason clearly about AI in Parts 33 and 34.
   368→   786→            </li>
   369→   787→        </ol>
   370→   788→
   371→   789→        <h2>1.7 Why Computer Science Matters</h2>
   372→   790→
   373→   791→        <p>
   374→   792→            Computing is now the backbone of nearly every field:
   375→   793→        </p>
   376→   794→
   377→   795→        <ul>
   378→   796→            <li><strong>Medicine</strong> — imaging, diagnostics, drug discovery.</li>
   379→   797→            <li><strong>Finance</strong> — payments, fraud detection, trading systems.</li>
   380→   798→            <li><strong>Communication</strong> — messaging, video calls, social platforms.</li>
   381→   799→            <li><strong>Science</strong> — climate modeling, genomics, space exploration.</li>
   382→   800→            <li><strong>Entertainment</strong> — games, streaming, recommendation systems.</li>
   383→   801→            <li><strong>Transportation</strong> — navigation, traffic control, autonomous vehicles.</li>
   384→   802→        </ul>
   385→   803→
   386→   804→        <p>
   387→   805→            For you personally, this foundation matters because every
   388→   806→            advanced role you might target — backend engineer, data
   389→   807→            scientist, ML engineer, AI agent engineer — is built on these
   390→   808→            concepts. Engineers who understand foundations learn new tools
   391→   809→            faster, debug deeper, and design better systems.
   392→   810→        </p>
   393→   811→
   394→   812→        <h2>1.8 Computer Science vs Related Fields</h2>
   395→   813→
   396→   814→        <p>
   397→   815→            Several fields overlap with Computer Science, and beginners often
   398→   816→            mix them up. Let's define each one before comparing them.
   399→   817→        </p>
   400→   818→
   401→   819→        <dl class="definitions">
   402→   820→            <dt>Computer Science (CS)</dt>
   403→   821→            <dd>
   404→   822→                The study of computation itself: algorithms, data, systems,
   405→   823→                and what can be automated. It is the theoretical and
   406→   824→                practical core from which the others grow.
   407→   825→            </dd>
   408→   826→
   409→   827→            <dt>Computer Engineering (CE)</dt>
   410→   828→            <dd>
   411→   829→                The design of physical computer systems: circuits, chips,
   412→   830→                processors, and the boundary where hardware meets software.
   413→   831→                A computer engineer might design the chip inside a phone.
   414→   832→            </dd>
   415→   833→
   416→   834→            <dt>Information Technology (IT)</dt>
   417→   835→            <dd>
   418→   836→                The use and management of computer systems to support
   419→   837→                organizations: setting up networks, maintaining servers,
   420→   838→                supporting users, keeping systems secure and running.
   421→   839→            </dd>
   422→   840→
   423→   841→            <dt>Software Engineering (SE)</dt>
   424→   842→            <dd>
   425→   843→                The disciplined practice of building reliable, maintainable
   426→   844→                software in teams: requirements, design, testing, version
   427→   845→                control, deployment, and maintenance. We cover its
   428→   846→                foundations in Part 26.
   429→   847→            </dd>
   430→   848→
   431→   849→            <dt>Data Science (DS)</dt>
   432→   850→            <dd>
   433→   851→                The practice of extracting knowledge from data, combining
   434→   852→                programming, statistics, and domain expertise — for example,
   435→   853→                analyzing customer behavior to predict sales.
   436→   854→            </dd>
   437→   855→
   438→   856→            <dt>Artificial Intelligence (AI)</dt>
   439→   857→            <dd>
   440→   858→                A branch of Computer Science focused on building systems that
   441→   859→                perform tasks normally requiring human-like intelligence:
   442→   860→                recognizing speech, making decisions, generating text.
   443→   861→                Machine learning is its most important modern approach.
   444→   862→            </dd>
   445→   863→        </dl>

   750→  1168→
   751→  1169→        <h2>Summary</h2>
   752→  1170→
   753→  1171→        <ul>
   754→  1172→            <li>Computer Science is the study of computation and problem solving, not merely the study of computers.</li>
   755→  1173→            <li>An algorithm is a precise, finite sequence of steps that solves a problem.</li>
   756→  1174→            <li>A computer accepts input, follows a stored program, and produces output.</li>
   757→  1175→            <li>Hardware is the physical machine; software is the instructions that run on it.</li>
   758→  1176→            <li>Computers excel at speed, repetition, storage, and communication, but they only follow instructions and have hard limits.</li>
   759→  1177→            <li>CS, Computer Engineering, IT, Software Engineering, Data Science, and AI are related but distinct fields.</li>
   760→  1178→            <li>The areas of CS stack on each other: theory → algorithms → programming → systems → applications.</li>
   761→  1179→        </ul>
