        <h2>1.1 What Is Computer Science?</h2>

        <p>
            The name is slightly misleading. Computer Science is not
            primarily the study of computers, just as astronomy is not the
            study of telescopes. Computers are the <em>tool</em>. The subject
            itself is about something deeper:
        </p>

        <div class="definition">
            <strong>Computer Science:</strong>
            <p>
                The study of <strong>computation</strong> — how problems can
                be solved by following precise, step-by-step procedures, and
                how those procedures can be carried out automatically by
                machines.
            </p>
        </div>

        <p>
            That definition contains two words we must explain immediately:
            <strong>computation</strong> and <strong>step-by-step
            procedures</strong>.
        </p>

        <p>
            <strong>Computation</strong> simply means: taking some input,
            following definite rules, and producing an output. When you add
            7 + 5 and get 12, you performed a computation. The input was
            "7 + 5", the rule was addition, and the output was 12.
        </p>

        <p>
            A <strong>step-by-step procedure</strong> for solving a problem
            has a technical name: an <strong>algorithm</strong>.
        </p>

        <div class="definition">
            <strong>Algorithm:</strong>
            <p>
                A precise, finite sequence of steps that solves a problem.
                Each step must be clear, and the procedure must eventually
                finish.
            </p>
        </div>

        <aside class="callout callout-analogy">
            <strong>Real-World Analogy</strong>
            <p>
                An algorithm is like a recipe. A recipe lists exact steps in
                an exact order: crack the eggs, whisk them, heat the pan,
                pour, cook. If someone follows the recipe precisely, they
                get the same result every time. An algorithm is a "recipe"
                for solving a problem — and unlike a human cook, a computer
                follows the recipe <em>exactly</em>, with no improvisation.
            </p>
        </aside>

        <p>
            So a more complete picture of Computer Science is:
        </p>

        <ul>
            <li><strong>Designing algorithms</strong> — finding good step-by-step solutions to problems.</li>
            <li><strong>Representing information</strong> — figuring out how numbers, text, images, and sound can be stored and processed.</li>
            <li><strong>Building systems</strong> — organizing hardware and software so computations happen reliably and efficiently.</li>
            <li><strong>Understanding limits</strong> — knowing what can be computed, what cannot, and how fast.</li>
        </ul>

        <aside class="callout callout-important">
            <strong>Key idea:</strong>
            Computer Science is fundamentally about
            <strong>problem solving</strong>. Programming (writing code) is
            one of its most important tools, but the thinking comes first.
            A person who can code but cannot design solutions is a tool
            operator; a computer scientist designs the solution and then
            uses code to express it.
        </aside>

        <h2>1.2 What Is Computing?</h2>

        <p>
            <strong>Computing</strong> is the act of performing computation
            — especially with the help of machines. Humans computed long
            before computers existed: accountants computed taxes, navigators
            computed positions using stars, and teams of people called
            "computers" (yes, that word once described a job title)
            calculated artillery tables by hand.
        </p>

        <p>
            Modern computing means <strong>automating</strong> that process:
            instead of a person doing each step, we record the steps once
            and let a fast, tireless machine repeat them billions of times
            per second.
        </p>

        <p>
            This is the core value of computing: once you turn a task into
            an algorithm, a machine can repeat it forever without getting
            tired, bored, or careless — as long as the instructions are
            correct.
        </p>

        <h2>1.3 What Is a Computer?</h2>

        <p>
            Now we can define the machine itself precisely.
        </p>

        <div class="definition">
            <strong>Computer:</strong>
            <p>
                An electronic machine that accepts <strong>data</strong>
                (input), follows stored instructions called a
                <strong>program</strong> to process that data, and produces
                results (output).
            </p>
        </div>

        <p>Four terms in that definition need immediate explanation:</p>

        <dl class="definitions">
            <dt>Data</dt>
            <dd>
                Raw facts and values: numbers, text, measurements, clicks,
                temperatures. Data by itself has no meaning yet — it becomes
                meaningful when processed. (We explore this fully in
                Part 4.)
            </dd>

            <dt>Input</dt>
            <dd>
                Any data entering the computer: a key press, a mouse
                movement, a voice recording, a temperature reading.
            </dd>

            <dt>Output</dt>
            <dd>
                Any result the computer produces: text on a screen, sound
                from a speaker, a sent message, a printed page.
            </dd>

            <dt>Program</dt>
            <dd>
                A stored list of instructions telling the computer what to
                do with the input, step by step.
            </dd>
        </dl>

        <p>Two more terms you will see constantly:</p>

        <dl class="definitions">
            <dt>Hardware</dt>
            <dd>
                The physical parts of a computer you can touch: chips,
                wires, screens, keyboards.
            </dd>

            <dt>Software</dt>
            <dd>
                The intangible instructions that run on the hardware:
                operating systems, apps, games. Software is "soft" because
                it can be changed without touching the machine physically.
            </dd>
        </dl>

        <p>
            Every computer, from the largest server to the smallest smart
            watch, follows the same basic pattern:
        </p>

        <pre class="ascii-diagram">
Input  →  Processing  →  Output
             ↑
          Storage
        </pre>

        <p>
            <strong>Storage</strong> is where data and programs are kept,
            either temporarily while working or permanently for later use.
            This simple diagram is the skeleton of every computer system you
            will ever study. Part 3 expands it in detail.
        </p>

        <aside class="callout callout-tip">
            <strong>Important:</strong>
            A computer is <em>not</em> only a laptop or desktop. The chips
            inside cars, televisions, washing machines, routers, and traffic
            lights are computers too. When a computer is built into another
            device to control it, we call it an
            <strong>embedded computer</strong> — "embedded" meaning built
            into something else.
        </aside>

        <h2>1.4 What Does a Computer Scientist Do?</h2>

        <p>
            Computer scientists use the ideas above to solve real problems.
            Their work typically falls into a few categories:
        </p>

        <ul>
            <li>
                <strong>Designing algorithms and software</strong> — creating
                the step-by-step logic behind search engines, games, banking
                systems, and medical tools.
            </li>
            <li>
                <strong>Building systems</strong> — making many computers and
                programs work together reliably: networks, databases, cloud
                platforms.
            </li>
            <li>
                <strong>Working with data and intelligence</strong> — building
                systems that learn patterns from data (machine learning) and
                systems that understand or generate language and images (AI).
            </li>
            <li>
                <strong>Research</strong> — discovering what is computable,
                how to make computation faster, safer, or smaller.
            </li>
            <li>
                <strong>Applying computing to other fields</strong> — biology
                (analyzing DNA), finance (modeling markets), climate science
                (simulating weather), art (generative tools).
            </li>
        </ul>

        <p>
            Notice that "typing code" is only a small visible slice of this
            work. Most of the real work happens <em>before</em> code:
            understanding the problem, designing a solution, and choosing
            the right structures and trade-offs.
        </p>

        <h2>1.5 What Can Computers Do?</h2>

        <p>Computers are extraordinarily good at a specific set of things:</p>

        <ul>
            <li>
                <strong>Arithmetic at extreme speed</strong> — billions of
                calculations per second.
            </li>
            <li>
                <strong>Storing and retrieving data</strong> — huge amounts,
                with near-perfect recall.
            </li>
            <li>
                <strong>Repeating tasks without fatigue</strong> — the same
                operation a million times with no loss of accuracy.
            </li>
            <li>
                <strong>Communication</strong> — moving data across the world
                in milliseconds.
            </li>
            <li>
                <strong>Automation</strong> — performing scheduled or
                triggered actions without human involvement.
            </li>
            <li>
                <strong>Simulation</strong> — modeling weather, crashes,
                economies, or neural networks before doing them in reality.
            </li>
            <li>
                <strong>Pattern recognition</strong> — with machine learning,
                finding patterns in data that are difficult to hand-code.
            </li>
        </ul>

        <h2>1.6 What Can Computers NOT Do?</h2>

        <p>
            Understanding limitations is just as important as understanding
            capabilities. Five limits matter most for beginners:
        </p>

        <ol>
            <li>
                <strong>Computers only follow instructions.</strong>
                If the instructions are wrong or ambiguous, the result is
                wrong. Computers do not "guess what you meant." There is a
                famous phrase for this: <em>garbage in, garbage out</em> —
                bad input or bad instructions produce bad results.
            </li>
            <li>
                <strong>Computers depend on the data they are given.</strong>
                A system trained on biased or incomplete data will produce
                biased or incomplete decisions.
            </li>
            <li>
                <strong>Some problems are unsolvable by any computer.</strong>
                At a basic level, certain questions are proven to have no
                possible algorithm that always answers them correctly. You
                will meet this idea if you later study the theory of
                computation. For now, just know: computation has hard
                mathematical limits.
            </li>
            <li>
                <strong>Physical limits exist.</strong>
                Signals cannot travel faster than light, chips produce heat,
                and components can only be made so small. These limits shape
                how fast computers can become.
            </li>
            <li>
                <strong>Computers do not truly understand.</strong>
                Modern AI can generate impressive text and images, but it
                works by recognizing and producing patterns in data. It does
                not have beliefs, intentions, or genuine understanding the
                way a person does. Keeping this distinction in mind will
                help you reason clearly about AI in Parts 33 and 34.
            </li>
        </ol>

        <h2>1.7 Why Computer Science Matters</h2>

        <p>
            Computing is now the backbone of nearly every field:
        </p>

        <ul>
            <li><strong>Medicine</strong> — imaging, diagnostics, drug discovery.</li>
            <li><strong>Finance</strong> — payments, fraud detection, trading systems.</li>
            <li><strong>Communication</strong> — messaging, video calls, social platforms.</li>
            <li><strong>Science</strong> — climate modeling, genomics, space exploration.</li>
            <li><strong>Entertainment</strong> — games, streaming, recommendation systems.</li>
            <li><strong>Transportation</strong> — navigation, traffic control, autonomous vehicles.</li>
        </ul>

        <p>
            For you personally, this foundation matters because every
            advanced role you might target — backend engineer, data
            scientist, ML engineer, AI agent engineer — is built on these
            concepts. Engineers who understand foundations learn new tools
            faster, debug deeper, and design better systems.
        </p>

        <h2>1.8 Computer Science vs Related Fields</h2>

        <p>
            Several fields overlap with Computer Science, and beginners often
            mix them up. Let's define each one before comparing them.
        </p>

        <dl class="definitions">
            <dt>Computer Science (CS)</dt>
            <dd>
                The study of computation itself: algorithms, data, systems,
                and what can be automated. It is the theoretical and
                practical core from which the others grow.
            </dd>

            <dt>Computer Engineering (CE)</dt>
            <dd>
                The design of physical computer systems: circuits, chips,
                processors, and the boundary where hardware meets software.
                A computer engineer might design the chip inside a phone.
            </dd>

            <dt>Information Technology (IT)</dt>
            <dd>
                The use and management of computer systems to support
                organizations: setting up networks, maintaining servers,
                supporting users, keeping systems secure and running.
            </dd>

            <dt>Software Engineering (SE)</dt>
            <dd>
                The disciplined practice of building reliable, maintainable
                software in teams: requirements, design, testing, version
                control, deployment, and maintenance. We cover its
                foundations in Part 26.
            </dd>

            <dt>Data Science (DS)</dt>
            <dd>
                The practice of extracting knowledge from data, combining
                programming, statistics, and domain expertise — for example,
                analyzing customer behavior to predict sales.
            </dd>

            <dt>Artificial Intelligence (AI)</dt>
            <dd>
                A branch of Computer Science focused on building systems that
                perform tasks normally requiring human-like intelligence:
                recognizing speech, making decisions, generating text.
                Machine learning is its most important modern approach.
            </dd>
        </dl>

        <aside class="callout callout-info">
            <strong>How they relate:</strong>
            Computer Science is the trunk of the tree. Computer Engineering
            builds the hardware it runs on; Software Engineering turns CS
            ideas into dependable products; IT operates those products;
            Data Science and AI are specialized branches growing out of CS.
            In practice, real jobs often blend several of these.
        </aside>

        <h2>1.9 The Major Areas of Computer Science</h2>

        <p>
            Inside Computer Science itself, work is organized into several
            major areas. You will meet every one of them in this course:
        </p>

        <ul>
            <li>
                <strong>Theory of computation</strong> — the mathematics of
                what can and cannot be computed, and how efficiently.
            </li>
            <li>
                <strong>Algorithms and data structures</strong> — efficient
                problem-solving procedures and the ways of organizing data
                (Parts 16–19).
            </li>
            <li>
                <strong>Programming languages</strong> — how humans write
                instructions computers can eventually execute (Parts 14–15).
            </li>
            <li>
                <strong>Computer systems</strong> — hardware architecture,
                operating systems, and networks (Parts 7–12, 20, 22).
            </li>
            <li>
                <strong>Databases</strong> — storing, organizing, and
                querying large amounts of data reliably (Part 24).
            </li>
            <li>
                <strong>Artificial intelligence and machine learning</strong>
                — systems that learn from data (Parts 33–34).
            </li>
            <li>
                <strong>Security</strong> — protecting systems and data from
                attacks and mistakes (Part 25).
            </li>
            <li>
                <strong>Human-computer interaction and graphics</strong> —
                how people experience and visualize computing.
            </li>
            <li>
                <strong>Software engineering</strong> — building software
                systematically in teams (Part 26).
            </li>
        </ul>

        <h2>1.10 How the Areas Connect</h2>

        <p>
            These areas are not isolated. They stack on each other:
        </p>

        <pre class="ascii-diagram">
Theory (what is computable?)
   ↓
Algorithms (how do we solve problems?)
   ↓
Programming languages (how do we express solutions?)
   ↓
Systems: OS, networks, databases (where do solutions run?)
   ↓
Applications: AI, security, web, mobile (what do we build?)
        </pre>

        <p>
            Every application at the top — an AI chatbot, a banking app, a
            video game — ultimately depends on every layer beneath it. When
            you understand the layers, complex systems stop being mysterious.
        </p>

    <section class="common-mistakes">

        <h2>Common Beginner Mistakes</h2>

        <ol>
            <li>
                <strong>Mistake:</strong>
                Believing Computer Science is the same as learning to code.

                <p>
                    <strong>Why it happens:</strong>
                    Coding is the most visible part of CS, so it looks like
                    the whole thing.
                </p>

                <p>
                    <strong>Better approach:</strong>
                    Treat coding as a language for expressing ideas. The
                    deeper skill is designing correct, efficient solutions —
                    which is what this course builds.
                </p>
            </li>

            <li>
                <strong>Mistake:</strong>
                Thinking a "computer" only means a laptop or desktop.

                <p>
                    <strong>Why it happens:</strong>
                    Those are the computers we see daily.
                </p>

                <p>
                    <strong>Better approach:</strong>
                    Remember the definition: any machine that accepts input,
                    follows a program, and produces output. Phones, routers,
                    cars, and smart appliances all qualify.
                </p>
            </li>

            <li>
                <strong>Mistake:</strong>
                Treating CS, IT, and Software Engineering as synonyms.

                <p>
                    <strong>Why it happens:</strong>
                    Job titles in the industry are often used loosely.
                </p>

                <p>
                    <strong>Better approach:</strong>
                    Use the comparison table in section 1.8. The fields
                    overlap in practice, but their core questions differ.
                </p>
            </li>

            <li>
                <strong>Mistake:</strong>
                Assuming AI systems "understand" like humans.

                <p>
                    <strong>Why it happens:</strong>
                    AI output looks human-like and fluent.
                </p>

                <p>
                    <strong>Better approach:</strong>
                    Remember: current AI finds and produces patterns from
                    data. That makes it powerful, but not conscious or
                    truly understanding. Parts 33–34 explain exactly how.
                </p>
            </li>
        </ol>

    <section class="tutorial-summary">

        <h2>Summary</h2>

        <ul>
            <li>Computer Science is the study of computation and problem solving, not merely the study of computers.</li>
            <li>An algorithm is a precise, finite sequence of steps that solves a problem.</li>
            <li>A computer accepts input, follows a stored program, and produces output.</li>
            <li>Hardware is the physical machine; software is the instructions that run on it.</li>
            <li>Computers excel at speed, repetition, storage, and communication, but they only follow instructions and have hard limits.</li>
            <li>CS, Computer Engineering, IT, Software Engineering, Data Science, and AI are related but distinct fields.</li>
            <li>The areas of CS stack on each other: theory → algorithms → programming → systems → applications.</li>
        </ul>

    </section>

    <section class="key-takeaways">

        <h2>Key Takeaways</h2>

        <ul>
            <li>Computation = input + rules + output.</li>
            <li>Algorithm = a recipe a machine can follow exactly.</li>
            <li>Computer = input → processing → output, with storage.</li>
            <li>Problem solving first, code second.</li>
            <li>Every advanced topic in this curriculum builds on these definitions.</li>
        </ul>
